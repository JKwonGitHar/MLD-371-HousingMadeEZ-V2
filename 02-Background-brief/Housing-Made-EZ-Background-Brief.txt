HOUSING MADE EZ
Background Brief: International Student Housing Navigation
MLD-371 AI and Civil Society | Harvard Kennedy School

Team: John Kwon, Elsa Nisa, Eze Chukwu, Mustafa Naqvi, Andrea Pavlick
Partner: International student groups at Harvard University


1. WHO WE ARE AND WHO WE SERVE

Housing Made EZ is an informal, ad hoc group of Harvard Kennedy School graduate students
enrolled in MLD-371. We are using AI to develop an informational tool that helps
international students affiliated with Harvard find and secure housing, on and off campus,
in Cambridge and the greater Boston area.

Our primary users are newly admitted international graduate and professional students
preparing to relocate, especially those unfamiliar with the U.S. rental system, without a
U.S. credit history, or without a trusted local network. Current international students
may also use the tool. Although it is designed for international students, all Harvard
students could benefit from it.

The population is large. According to Harvard's Office for Institutional Research &
Analytics, Harvard enrolled 6,749 U.S. nonresident students across all programs in 2025.
Most were in graduate programs, including 555 at the Harvard Kennedy School. We began with
issues raised by a small number of HKS international students and will widen our focus as
we hear from more of the graduate community.

2. WHO RUNS IT AND HOW DECISIONS ARE MADE

The group is run by its five members, all HKS graduate students. We have no budget. We make
decisions through informed discussion and aim for consensus; if we cannot reach consensus,
we decide by majority vote.

We are not working with a single board or office. We engage several student communities:
Edward S. Mason Program fellows, HKS clubs and organizations, graduate students at the
Harvard Kenneth C. Griffin Graduate School of Arts and Sciences, and Harvard WhatsApp groups.
The tool is meant to supplement, not replace, the support Harvard University Housing provides.

3. THE PROBLEM AND THE RECURRING TASKS

International students face the same challenges as anyone moving to Boston, but with less
context. They often do not know the local rental market, which sources to trust, or how to
spot a scam, and rental scams are a documented problem in Boston (Boston.gov; Boston Police
Department). Housing becomes a series of high-stakes decisions: where to search, whether a
listing or landlord is legitimate, which documents are accepted without U.S. credit history,
what a lease means, and what to do when plans change.

The recurring, day-to-day tasks students must complete include:
  - finding housing and roommates, often before arriving in the U.S.;
  - understanding, reviewing, and signing legally binding leases;
  - avoiding rental scams and verifying listings and landlords;
  - applying without a U.S. credit history (guarantors, deposits, proof of funds);
  - opening a bank account to pay rent, deposits, and utilities;
  - setting up and paying for utilities;
  - finding, buying, and later selling furniture when moving in and out; and
  - arranging subleases or temporary housing when schedules do not match the academic year.

Some groups face extra difficulty. Mason Fellows arrive off-cycle in July, a hard time to
find housing, and students with families must also enroll children in U.S. schools. Other
issues we hope to address include affordability, access to the private rental market,
navigating the Harvard University Housing system, Boston versus Cambridge trade-offs, and
transportation.

Out of scope: immigration and visa issues, and anything related to Harvard enrollment.

4. WHAT STUDENTS USE TODAY

To our understanding, students currently combine:
  - WhatsApp, Slack, Telegram, and other peer networks;
  - the Harvard University Housing site and institutional listings;
  - public marketplaces such as Zillow, Craigslist, and Facebook;
  - search engines and, we assume, AI-assisted search; and
  - advice from friends, classmates, alumni, landlords, and brokers.

These resources help, but they are scattered and unevenly trusted. The gaps are unclear
credibility, stale or incomplete information, no clear sequence from search to application
to lease signing, little help comparing options or spotting red flags, and no obvious place
to turn when a student is unsure or at risk.

5. HOW WE ARE GATHERING INFORMATION AND USING AI

We are running an anonymous survey of international students, collecting only what we need
to identify concerns, and will keep engaging students throughout the project. Early
responses point to four common pain points: apartment scams, understanding leases, setting
up bank accounts, and understanding utility costs. We will update this list as more
responses arrive.

We are using AI to (a) analyze survey responses, (b) assess whether any use of social-media
data, such as WhatsApp discussions, would raise privacy concerns, and (c) help decide which
type of tool will be most useful. We will not use any method that jeopardizes students'
privacy, and we are paying attention to the privacy, legal, ethical, and governance
questions raised by using AI on a collective-action problem.

6. PRODUCT DIRECTION AND NEXT STEPS

Our working hypothesis is that international students do not need more listings. They need
a trusted way to understand their options, recognize risks, prepare applications, and know
where to get help. The first prototype will test a narrow "Housing Decision Navigator" that
lets a student:
  1. describe their situation, timing, budget, preferred location, and housing type;
  2. get a tailored sequence of search channels and preparation steps;
  3. check a listing against a red-flag and verification checklist;
  4. prepare questions and documents for applications and landlord conversations;
  5. see which lease issues need professional or institutional help; and
  6. reach a trusted Harvard or community resource when the tool cannot safely answer.

The first version will not be a marketplace, guarantee that a listing is legitimate, give
legal advice, or store sensitive documents. Furniture exchange, utilities, banking, and
sublease matching may follow as later modules. We have already built an early resource page
covering scams, leases, banking, rent comparisons, roommates, and childcare.

Next steps: finish the survey, identify the most frequent and most serious decision point,
build the smallest trusted tool that addresses it, and test it with international students
to gather feedback. Key questions before building: which student segment and housing moment
to serve first, what information we can verify and keep current, when the tool must refer
users elsewhere, and whether a better-organized layer over existing resources would work
better than a new interface.
